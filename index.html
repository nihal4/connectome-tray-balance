A robot arm run by the wiring of a fly's brain

The arm sees the tray through a camera wired into a fruit fly's eye neurons, feels its joints through the fly's body-sense neurons, and moves through the fly's command neurons. Everything in between is the real connection map of the fly brain.

Code on GitHub Run it yourself by S. M. Nihal Ahmed

How the robot is wired

The fly brain has been mapped neuron by neuron: about 139,000 neurons and 15 million connections (FlyWire). For this robot I take 2,500 of them that lie on the real pathways from the eye to the brain's command neurons, keep who connects to whom and whether each connection excites or inhibits, and train only the connection strengths with reinforcement learning.

Each camera pixel drives exactly one photoreceptor (R8 cells get the current frame, R7 cells the previous one, so motion is visible). The arm's joint readings go into ascending neurons, the channel that normally carries body sensation up to the fly's brain. The arm is driven from descending neurons, the brain's outgoing commands. A small learned layer translates those commands into the arm's five motors.

What happened, run by run

A record of the experiments so far, including the ones that went badly. All are single runs, so read the numbers as first results, not a benchmark.

  1. Balancing a ball, given the ball's position as numbers

    The first network used the 1,000 most connected fly neurons with inputs and outputs picked by me, not by biology. It learned to keep a rolling ball on a flat tray in about 100,000 steps and stayed robust to noise, pushes and a heavier ball. It only failed when the ball was hidden from it. An easy start, so the tasks got harder.

  2. A tilt-maze puzzle

    Like the wooden labyrinth toy: roll the ball around the walls to the green goal and stop it there. The network gets the wall layout but not the route. Along the way it found two shortcuts I had to close: flicking the ball over the walls (now there is a glass lid) and hovering in and out of the goal to collect reward (now the reward for resting is taken back if the ball leaves).

    On one fixed maze the fly-wired network learns slowly: its first solves appear after a few hundred thousand steps and it reached 30 to 40 % by a million steps, still rising. It has not learned to solve new, random mazes yet.

  3. Wired like a fly, seeing with a camera

    Then the robot was rewired through the fly's own channels, as in the diagram above, and the ball's position was taken away: the network only gets the camera image and its joints. In the full run (1 million steps on Kaggle, about 3 hours) it held the ball for a second or two at first, then from 250,000 steps on it kept it for the full 10 seconds and stayed there.

  4. The maze, seen only through the camera: solved

    The same fly-robot brain then had to solve the tilt maze with nothing but its eye: see the walls, the goal and the ball in a 16×16 picture, find the way round and stop the ball in the goal.

    First try (fast motors): it learned the route and rolled the ball to the goal in under a second, but pinned it in the corner of the goal cell instead of stopping it in the middle, so no maze counted as solved, even after 2 million steps.

    Second try: slower, smoother motors (like servos with a speed limit, so the ball moves about three times slower) and practice starts partway along the route. Nothing in the brain changed. First solve at 350,000 steps, every test maze solved from 1.1 million steps, and by 2 million it finishes in about 2.6 seconds and stops the ball in the centre.

  5. Next: a drone with a fly's compound eyes

    A fly's brain evolved to steer flight, so the next robot is a small quadcopter. It sees through two compound eyes of 160 facets each, covering almost every direction, each facet a blurry spot as in a real fly. Every facet feeds its own three photoreceptors in the fly wiring: R1–6 (brightness, the input to motion and looming detection), R7 (ultraviolet) and R8 (green). A simple stabiliser keeps the drone level, the job a fly's halteres do outside the brain. The fly-brain network (4,000 neurons) decides where to fly.

    First task: a yellow ball appears somewhere new in a room each time; find it by eye, fly to it and hover on it. Training already includes wind, gusts, a noisy eye, a slightly different drone each time and blue decoy balls, so there is nothing to memorise and no perfect world to get used to.

    Checked before training: a hand-written pilot that sees only what the compound eye sees finds and reaches the ball in 83 % of rooms, so the eye carries enough information; with the ball made invisible it never succeeds, and hovering or random flying solve none. A first version painted the floor green: the yellow ball was camouflaged against it, much as a flower can be in grass, so the room is now grey. Results will be added here.

What is biological here, and what is not

PartStatus
Which neurons are used (eye, body sense, commands)Real fly cell types
Who connects to whom, excitatory or inhibitoryReal wiring
Route from eye through optic lobe and central brain to commandsReal pathways
Connection strengthsStart from real synapse counts, then retrained
NeuronsSimple rate units, not spiking cells
The eye's inputArm: a 16×16 grey image. Drone: a fly-like compound eye with real photoreceptor types, but 160 instead of ~800 facets per eye and 25 instead of ~200 images per second
BalanceDrone: a simple stabiliser stands in for the halteres and nerve cord, which the brain map does not include
LearningReinforcement learning (PPO), not how a fly learns

So this is not a simulated fly mind. It uses the fly brain's circuit layout as the design of a robot controller, and asks how well that design can learn tasks a fly never does.

Run it yourself

Everything runs on a laptop (with a safe low-power mode for an 8 GB Mac) or on a free Kaggle notebook (notebooks/kaggle_flyrobot.ipynb).

git clone https://github.com/nihal4/connectome-tray-balance
cd connectome-tray-balance
pip install -r requirements.txt

python scripts/download_connectome.py     # fly wiring + cell types
python scripts/build_flybrain.py          # eye-to-command brain, 2,500 neurons
python scripts/train.py --env tray_vision --policy connectome \
       --subset data/flybrain_cam16_N2500.npz --profile kaggle

For the camera maze, set ENV = "maze_fixed3_vision" in the notebook (the current default).

Data: FlyWire connectome v783 (Dorkenwald et al. 2024; Schlegel et al. 2024, Nature), processed edge list from Shiu et al. 2024 (Nature), cell types from flywire_annotations. Simulation: MuJoCo. Training: Stable-Baselines3 PPO.

Related work: FLYNN (Wang & Chen 2026), FlyGM (2026), Lappalainen et al. 2024.
